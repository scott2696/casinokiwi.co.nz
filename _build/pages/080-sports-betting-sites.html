The markets, and where the money actually is

Head to head

Pick the winner. The simplest market and the one with the tightest margin, because it carries the most liquidity and books cannot afford to be wrong on it. A reasonable place to start and a poor place to look for an edge.

Line and handicap

One side starts with a virtual points advantage, turning a lopsided contest into a roughly even-money bet. The standard rugby and league market in New Zealand. Margins are tight on the main line and noticeably looser on alternate lines, which is where the value tends to sit.

Totals

Combined points, runs or goals over or under a set number, independent of who wins. Useful when you have a view on conditions — weather, a slow deck, a referee — without a view on the result.

Player props

First try scorer, most tackles, runs scored, most disposals. The softest markets at almost every book, because they are the hardest to price and attract the least sharp money. If you actually watch the sport, this is where knowledge converts into edge. It is also where books limit winning accounts fastest.

Multis and same-game multis

Several legs combined; every one must win. The odds multiply and so does the margin on each leg — a four-leg multi routinely carries 15% or more of built-in overround against roughly 4% on a single. Same-game multis price correlated legs conservatively on top of that. Entertaining, structurally poor value.

Futures

Season-long markets — NPC winner, Super Rugby champion, NRL premiership. Money is tied up for months with no ability to compound it, so treat a futures bet as an entertainment position rather than an investment.

In-play

Betting during the match with live odds. The best books add cash-out, which lets you take a guaranteed return before the event finishes — always at a price that slightly favours the book. Useful for managing a position, expensive as a habit.

Reading the margin before you bet

Every price contains the book's cut. Finding it takes ten seconds and tells you more about a bookmaker than any review.

  1. Convert each price to implied probability: 1 divided by the decimal odds.
  2. Add them up across every outcome in the market.
  3. The amount above 100% is the overround — the book's margin.

Limiting: what happens if you win

Rarely discussed and worth knowing before it happens to you. Bookmakers manage risk by restricting customers who beat them, and offshore books do it faster and with less warning than regulated ones.

  • Stake factoring. Your maximum bet on a market quietly drops — sometimes to a few dollars — while other customers see normal limits.
  • Bonus exclusion. Promotions stop appearing in your account.
  • Delayed acceptance. Bets go to manual review before being accepted, by which time the price has moved.
  • Account closure. The end point, usually with a balance return and no explanation.

None of this is against the rules and all of it is in the terms. It disproportionately affects exactly the markets where knowledge pays — player props and niche competitions. Practical response: hold accounts at several books, and do not build a balance at one you may lose access to.

The legal position, stated plainly

What the 2025 law actually did

Legislation in force from July 2025 gave TAB NZ — operating alongside Betcha, run under an Entain arrangement — exclusive rights to offer sports and racing betting to New Zealanders, and barred offshore bookmakers from accepting New Zealand business. Enforcement is directed at operators, not at individual punters: there is no offence for a New Zealand resident who places a bet with an international book, and no penalty has been applied to a player. What changes is your recourse. If a dispute arises with an offshore bookmaker, you are relying on a foreign regulator rather than a New Zealand one, and you have no access to the domestic complaints framework that covers TAB.

This is separate from casino law. The Online Casino Gambling Act is creating a licensed domestic casino market with up to 15 DIA licences; sports and racing betting sits under the TAB framework instead. The two regimes are frequently confused, including by sites that should know better.

Sports betting NZ: FAQ

Is online sports betting legal in New Zealand?

TAB NZ, alongside Betcha, holds the only legal right to offer sports and racing betting to New Zealanders under legislation in force from July 2025, and offshore bookmakers are barred from accepting New Zealand business. The legal duty sits with operators rather than punters, so individual New Zealanders are not penalised for using international sites, but disputes with an offshore book fall outside the New Zealand complaints framework.

Is TAB the only legal bookmaker in NZ?

Yes. TAB NZ and Betcha are the only bookmakers legally permitted to take sports and racing bets from New Zealand residents. TAB also returns funds to the domestic racing industry, and its coverage of New Zealand thoroughbred, harness and greyhound meetings is deeper than any international operator's.

Do I pay tax on betting winnings in New Zealand?

No. Inland Revenue does not treat recreational gambling winnings as assessable income, so sports and racing winnings are yours in full with no income tax and no GST. The exceptions are gambling carried on as a business and cryptocurrency gains, since crypto is treated as property.

Which sportsbook has the best odds for Kiwi punters?

It depends on the sport. TAB prices New Zealand racing and NPC rugby better than anyone because it holds the deepest domestic liquidity. International books generally price overseas football, NBA, esports and player prop markets more competitively, and offer far more market types per fixture.

How fast do sportsbooks pay out?

Settlement usually happens within minutes of an event finishing. Getting the money out is the slower part: crypto withdrawals clear in ten minutes to four hours, e-wallets in two to twenty-four hours, and card or bank transfers in one to five business days.

Which betting markets offer the best value?

Player props across rugby, league and cricket, because they are the hardest for bookmakers to price and attract the least sharp money. Head-to-head markets carry the tightest margins but also the least pricing error. Multis are the worst value — a four-leg multi routinely carries 15% or more of built-in margin against about 4% on a single bet.

How do I work out a bookmaker's margin?

Convert every price in the market to implied probability by dividing 1 by the decimal odds, then add them up. Anything above 100% is the margin. A two-way market priced 1.91 each side carries about 4.7%; the same market at 1.95 carries 2.6%. Over a season that difference is worth more than any tipping service.

Why did my betting account get limited?

Bookmakers restrict customers who beat them, usually by quietly cutting your maximum stake, removing promotions, or routing your bets to manual review before acceptance. It is in the terms and it happens fastest on player props and niche markets. Hold accounts at several books and avoid building a balance at one you could lose access to.

Is TAB better than international bookmakers for NZ racing?

Substantially, and it is not close. TAB covers every New Zealand thoroughbred, harness and greyhound meeting with full fields, form, scratchings, tote pools and exotics such as trifectas. International books price feature days at best and rarely offer exotics at all. For overseas sport, player props and in-play tools the international books are stronger.

What is cash-out and should I use it?

It lets you settle a bet before the event finishes for a guaranteed amount, always priced slightly in the bookmaker's favour. It is useful for managing a specific position — a leg has landed and you want certainty — but using it habitually converts the book's margin into a recurring cost on top of the one you already pay.
